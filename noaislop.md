# No AI Slop

The goal of this skill is not to pass an AI detector. It is to write like a specific, informed person who knows the subject and respects the reader. AI slop is what you get when prose optimizes for sounding competent instead of being useful: it is smooth, agreeable, evenly weighted, and empty. Real writing has friction, opinions, specifics, and a point of view.

Read the two halves of this skill together. The first half is the failure modes to avoid. The second half is what good writing actually does, because removing tics without adding substance just gives you bland prose with fewer em-dashes.

## How to use this skill

Write the draft with these principles in mind, then do a dedicated **editing pass** where you hunt specifically for the patterns below. Most slop is added in the drafting reflex and only removed on purpose. When editing someone else's text or your own earlier draft, read it as a skeptical human would and fix the specific offending sentences rather than rewriting wholesale, since good sentences should survive.

If the user has given you their own writing rules (past instructions, a style guide, examples of their voice), those **override** anything here they conflict with. This skill is the floor, not the ceiling.

## Part 1: Patterns to avoid (the 50+)

### A. Punctuation and mechanics

1. **Em-dashes as a default connector.** The single most recognizable tell. Do not use the em-dash (—) to join clauses, insert asides, or create dramatic pauses. Restructure into two sentences, or use a comma, colon, or parentheses. A human writer reaches for an em-dash rarely and on purpose; AI litters them everywhere. Default to zero.
2. **Curly/smart quotes and other copy-paste artifacts** ("" '') appearing uniformly, which flag a machine source. Match whatever the destination uses; when unsure, use straight quotes.
3. **The "rule of three" reflex.** Adding a third item to every list because triads sound rhythmic: "creative, smart, and funny," "innovative, transformative, and groundbreaking." Real emphasis is uneven. Use one adjective, or two, or a list of five, when that is what is true.
4. **Perfectly parallel sentence structure** repeated across a paragraph, where every sentence has the same shape and length. Vary rhythm. Follow a long sentence with a short one. Let one fragment stand alone.
5. **Over-hedged qualifiers stacked together**: "it's worth noting that it may, in some cases, potentially..." Say the thing or don't.
6. **Title Case On Every Heading and random Bolding** of Key Phrases mid-sentence to signal importance. Bold sparingly; let the words carry the weight.
7. **Emoji as section bullets or tone-softeners** (✅ 🚀 💡) unless the user's channel actually uses them.

### B. Signature words and phrases (the vocabulary tells)

8. **"Delve"** and its cousins: "dive into," "unpack," "explore the world of." Just say "look at" or start explaining.
9. **"It's not just X, it's Y"** and **"It's not about X, it's about Y."** The signature AI contrast frame. It sounds profound and says little. State what it *is*.
10. **"A is X. B is not."** / **"Gone are the days of..."** The negative-parallelism setup. (User has flagged this one directly across projects.) Rewrite as a direct claim.
11. **"In today's fast-paced world / digital age / ever-evolving landscape."** Dead opener. Start with the actual subject.
12. **"Whether you're a X or a Y"** audience-spanning intros that try to include everyone and land nowhere.
13. **"Navigate," "leverage," "utilize," "facilitate," "streamline," "harness," "unlock," "elevate," "empower," "foster," "spearhead," "underscore," "showcase," "boast."** Corporate filler verbs. Use "use," "help," "cut," "raise," "show," "run."
14. **"Robust, seamless, cutting-edge, comprehensive, holistic, dynamic, innovative, transformative, game-changing, next-level, world-class, best-in-class, state-of-the-art."** Marketing adjectives that assert quality instead of demonstrating it.
15. **"Tapestry," "realm," "landscape," "ecosystem," "journey," "symphony," "beacon," "cornerstone," "testament to."** Overwrought metaphor nouns.
16. **"Crucial, vital, essential, pivotal, paramount, key."** Importance-inflation words applied to everything.
17. **"Moreover, furthermore, additionally, notably, importantly, indeed, thus, hence."** Stiff transition words humans rarely say aloud. Prefer "and," "but," "so," "also," or no transition at all.
18. **"It's important to note that," "it's worth mentioning," "keep in mind that," "as we can see."** Filler throat-clearing. Delete and start with the content.
19. **"When it comes to X..."** A wind-up that adds nothing. Start with X.
20. **"That being said," "at the end of the day," "needless to say."** Verbal packing peanuts.
21. **"Not only... but also..."** Usually just a padded "and."
22. **"Plays a crucial role in," "plays a significant part."** Vague causal filler. Say what it actually does.
23. **"In the realm of," "in the world of," "in the context of."** Prepositional padding before the noun.
24. **"Ranging from X to Y"** as a fake-specific gesture that lists two random things.
25. **"Fast-paced, rapidly evolving, ever-changing, increasingly."** Change-inflation adverbs.

### C. Rhetorical and structural tics

26. **The "isn't just / more than just" reveal** followed by an inflated claim. "This isn't just a tool, it's a movement."
27. **Rhetorical questions the writer immediately answers.** "So what does this mean for you? It means..." Just tell them.
28. **The obligatory "In conclusion / In summary / To wrap up" paragraph** that restates the article with no new value. End on a real thought instead.
29. **Restating the prompt or heading in the first line of a section.** If the H2 says "Benefits of X," don't open with "There are many benefits of X."
30. **"Let's dive in," "let's explore," "buckle up," "grab a coffee."** Manufactured enthusiasm openers.
31. **Symmetry everywhere:** intro-3points-conclusion, every section the same length, every list exactly parallel. Real arguments are lopsided; the important part gets more room.
32. **The "two-part title with a colon" reflex** on everything: "Mastering X: A Comprehensive Guide to Y."
33. **False balance / both-sidesing** where the writer refuses to land on a position: "there are pros and cons to consider." Take a stance.
34. **Ending every section with a forward-reference:** "But that's only half the story..." / "Now let's turn to..." Used once it's fine; as a reflex it's filler.
35. **Numbered-list-of-everything.** Converting prose that should flow into bullets because lists feel organized. Bullets are for genuinely parallel items, not for avoiding paragraphs. (The user's channels usually want prose, not bullet-heavy pages.)
36. **The "here's the kicker / and here's the thing / but here's the truth" fake-suspense** transition.

### D. Tone and stance (the emptiness tells)

37. **Puffery and editorializing importance:** "This groundbreaking approach is a testament to the power of..." Undue emphasis on how significant/symbolic something is. Show the thing; let the reader judge its importance.
38. **Relentless positivity and agreeableness.** Never pushing back, never saying something is a bad idea, never noting a tradeoff. Real expertise includes "don't do this" and "this part is weak."
39. **Vague universal claims with no owner:** "studies show," "experts agree," "it is widely known." Name the study, the expert, the source, or drop the claim.
40. **Hedging that removes all commitment:** "may potentially help in some cases depending on various factors." Say what you actually believe and mark real uncertainty honestly.
41. **Fabricated specificity:** confident stats, dates, or quotes that were never verified. If you didn't check it, don't state it as fact; flag it for verification.
42. **Praising the reader/topic to fill space:** "Great question!" "This is such an important topic." Cut it.
43. **Corporate neutrality** where a real person would have texture, humor, irritation, or a strong preference. Voice is what's missing.
44. **Explaining the obvious** at length to hit a word count, defining terms the audience already knows.
45. **Both-hands conclusions:** "Ultimately, the choice depends on your specific needs and goals." A non-answer. Give your actual recommendation.

### E. Substance and evidence tells

46. **Confident but shallow:** grammatically perfect prose that never gets past the surface of the topic. If a knowledgeable reader learns nothing they couldn't guess, it's slop regardless of polish.
47. **No concrete detail:** no names, numbers, examples, screenshots, dollar figures, dates, or first-hand specifics. Generic content that could be about any company in any industry.
48. **No lived experience / no E-E-A-T:** no "when I did this," no real case, no "here's what went wrong." (The user consistently asks for a real example or named practitioner to ground a claim.)
49. **Wikipedia-voice summary** of a topic instead of a take on it. Aggregating what's already known adds nothing; the value is in synthesis, judgment, and what's *not* obvious.
50. **Filler that survives deletion.** The concision test: if you can remove a sentence or phrase and lose no meaning, it was slop. (The user's standing preference: if you can cut words and keep the point, cut them.)
51. **Forced or irrelevant links/CTAs** dropped in because "content should link out," breaking intent-match. A link belongs only where a real reader would want it. Don't bolt one on.
52. **Keyword stuffing and repetition** of the target phrase past the point a human would say it.
53. **Answering a different, easier question** than the one asked because it's safer, instead of the specific hard thing the reader needs.
54. **Over-explaining your own structure:** "In this article, we will first cover A, then B, then C." Just cover them.

## Part 2: What good writing does (add these, don't just subtract)

Removing tics is half the job. These are the moves that make prose read as a real, informed person wrote it.

**Prepare the reader before you instruct or assert.** Don't open a section with a cold command or a strange abstract phrase. Name the reader's situation or the reason first, then deliver the mechanic. Instead of "Match your symptom to the branch below," open with the instinct the reader has, why it's wrong, then give the instruction. This "warm the opener" move is one the user has flagged repeatedly: every section should ease the reader in before it directs them.

**Lead with the point, then support it.** Say the useful thing first. Don't wind up with throat-clearing before the substance.

**Be specific and concrete.** Names, numbers, real examples, actual dollar figures, real dates, first-hand detail. One concrete example beats three abstract sentences. When a claim needs authority, ground it in a real study, a named person, or a real result (E-E-A-T), the way the user asks for a real practitioner or case to anchor an argument.

**Have a point of view.** Take a position. Say what you'd do, what you'd avoid, what's overrated. Push back when something is a bad idea. A willingness to say "don't do this" is what separates an expert from an aggregator.

**Vary rhythm deliberately.** Mix long and short sentences. Let a two-word sentence land. Read it aloud in your head; if every sentence has the same shape, break the pattern.

**Match the channel and voice.** A sales page, a diagnostic article, and a text to a friend are not the same register. If the user has a voice (from past work or a style profile), write in it. Default to warm, direct, and human over corporate-neutral.

**Cut until it hurts, then stop.** After drafting, delete every word that survives deletion without loss. Concision is the strongest single signal of a careful human writer. This is the user's core standing instruction: if the point survives with fewer words, use fewer words.

**Structure should follow the argument, not a template.** The important part gets more room. Skip the section that has nothing to say. Don't force intro-body-conclusion symmetry onto ideas that are naturally lopsided.

**End on something real.** A last thought, a recommendation, an honest caveat, a specific next step. Never a hollow "in conclusion" restatement.

**Flag what you couldn't verify.** If a stat or fact is unconfirmed, say so rather than stating it confidently. Honest uncertainty reads as human and trustworthy; fabricated confidence is the deepest slop.

## Editing checklist (run this pass before delivering)

Do a fast, targeted sweep for the highest-signal offenders:

- **Search for the em-dash character (—) and remove every one.** Non-negotiable default.
- Search for: delve, leverage, utilize, seamless, robust, crucial, vital, tapestry, realm, journey, landscape, testament, elevate, unlock, navigate, foster, moreover, furthermore, "it's worth noting," "it's important to note," "in today's," "when it comes to," "not just," "isn't just," "in conclusion." Kill or replace each hit.
- Find every "rule of three" list and ask if the third item is real or padding.
- Find every rhetorical question and either cut it or replace it with the answer.
- Find every sentence that could be deleted with no loss of meaning, and delete it.
- Check that at least one concrete specific (name, number, example, real result) appears where a claim is made.
- Check that the piece takes a position somewhere instead of both-sidesing.
- Confirm the opener prepares the reader rather than issuing a cold command.
- Confirm any link or CTA is where a real reader would want it, not bolted on.
- Read the first line of each section: does it restate the heading? Rewrite it.

If you fixed more than a handful of these, do one more read, because slop clusters.

---

## A note on judgment

None of these patterns is banned in the abstract. A human writer occasionally uses an em-dash, a triad, or the word "crucial," and that's fine. The tell is not any single instance; it's the *reflex* of reaching for these by default, in density, across a whole piece. Write like a specific person who has something real to say, and most of this takes care of itself. The list is for the editing pass, when you hunt down the reflexes the drafting brain left behind.
